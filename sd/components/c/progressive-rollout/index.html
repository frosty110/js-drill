Progressive Rollout

Earning a change's blast radius in stages — run it with no effect, then advisory, then live on a narrow slice — so each widening is justified by evidence the previous stage produced.

Reach for it when

  • The change alters a decision rather than an implementation, so correctness cannot be established by a test suite alone
  • Being wrong is expensive or irreversible, and you would rather find out on 1% than on everyone
  • You need labelled evidence that the change is good, and the earlier stages generate exactly that data

Don't reach for it when

  • The change is trivially reversible and cheap to get wrong, where staging is ceremony that slows the loop
  • You cannot measure the outcome at a slice, since a rollout you cannot evaluate is just a slower deploy

What it costs you

  • Two code paths live simultaneously, and the shadow path costs real compute while producing nothing a user sees
  • Comparison infrastructure is the actual work: without a diff between old and new outcomes, the stages tell you nothing
  • Someone has to own the decision to widen, or a rollout parks at 5% indefinitely and becomes permanent complexity

How it breaks

  • Advancing on elapsed time rather than on evidence, which is a scheduled deploy wearing a rollout's clothes
  • A slice that is not representative — internal users, one region, the cheapest traffic — so the signal never resembles production
  • No ratchet back: widening is automated and narrowing is a manual scramble during the incident
  • Shadow output that is never compared, which pays the full cost of the mechanism for none of the benefit

Instead, consider

  • Circuit Breaker — Reacts to a failure that is already happening; a rollout bounds how much traffic could have been exposed to it.
  • Bulkhead Isolation — Contains blast radius by partitioning resources rather than by limiting how much traffic sees the change.
