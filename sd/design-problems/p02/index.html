Design a News Feed

A Twitter/Facebook-style home feed that shows each user a merged, ranked stream of posts from the accounts they follow. The signature challenge is the fan-out tradeoff: precompute feeds on write (push) vs. assemble them on read (pull), and the hybrid needed to survive high-follower 'celebrity' accounts.

Results carried by this link

Components in play 6

  • Cache signature — The per-user timeline of post IDs in Redis, hydrated to full post bodies on read. The cache IS the precomputed feed, not an optimisation over it.
  • Hot-Key Mitigation signature — The celebrity account: one write with tens of millions of followers, which is precisely the case that breaks naive fan-out-on-write.
  • Fan-out signature — The central tradeoff: push each post into every follower's timeline on write, or assemble on read — with the hybrid as the production answer.
  • Ranking signature — Orders the assembled timeline by recency, affinity and engagement rather than pure chronology — the second half of the feed problem after fan-out.
  • GraphQL — Lets the client fetch posts, authors and engagement counts in one round trip instead of three, which matters most on mobile.
  • Cursor Pagination — Cursor-based feed paging, so posts arriving mid-scroll do not shift the window and duplicate what the user just read.

The brief

Functional requirements

  • Post content; follow/unfollow; load a home feed merging followees' posts, ranked, infinite-scroll.
  • Out of v1 scope: DMs, ads, notifications.
  • Eventual consistency is fine — a post may appear seconds late.

Scale & constraints

  • 300M DAU · ~200 followees each · 2 posts/day · 10 feed opens/day
  • ~7K post writes/s (14K peak) · ~35K feed reads/s (70K peak)
  • Pure push fan-out → 120B feed-writes/day (~1.4M/s): a 200× amplification
  • Follower distribution is power-law — the celebrity tail drives the hard decision; <200ms feed load

Key ideas

  • Fan-out-on-write (push) precomputes each follower's feed at post time — fast reads, but write amplification explodes for high-follower accounts.
  • Fan-out-on-read (pull) assembles the feed at query time by merging followees' recent posts — cheap writes, but slow, repeated reads.
  • The production answer is HYBRID: push for normal users, pull for celebrities, merged at read time.
  • Store each user's feed as a capped, per-user timeline of post IDs in a fast store (e.g. Redis) — hydrate post bodies on read.
  • Ranking can be reverse-chronological or a scored model (recency, affinity, engagement); chrono is simpler, scored drives engagement.
  • Paginate with a cursor (last-seen post ID / timestamp), not OFFSET, so the feed is stable as new posts arrive.
  • A celebrity post is a thundering-herd write event — decouple fan-out via a queue and never push to millions synchronously.

Q1 explain & apply

Frame the problem: what are the functional and non-functional requirements for a home news feed, and the read:write characteristics?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • Functional: post content; follow/unfollow users; view a home feed merging followees' posts; feed is ranked (chrono or scored); infinite-scroll pagination.
  • Non-functional: low feed-load latency (<200ms), high availability, eventual consistency is acceptable (a post can appear seconds late).
  • Heavily read-skewed — users scroll far more than they post; peak read QPS dominates.
  • Scale assumptions to confirm: DAU, avg followees per user, avg posts/user/day, follower distribution (power-law with celebrities).
  • Out of scope for v1: DMs, ads, notifications — confirm.
Model answer: Functionally: users post, follow/unfollow, and load a home feed that merges recent posts from everyone they follow, ranked and infinitely scrollable. Non-functionally it's read-dominated — people scroll far more than they post — so I optimize feed-read latency (sub-200ms) and high availability, and I'll accept eventual consistency since a post appearing a few seconds late is fine. The numbers I'd confirm are DAU, average followees, posts per user per day, and crucially the follower distribution, because the power-law tail (celebrities) drives the hardest design decision.

Q2 explain & apply

Estimate capacity. Assume 300M DAU, each user follows ~200 accounts, posts 2×/day, and opens the feed 10×/day. Derive post write QPS, feed read QPS, and the fan-out write volume under a pure push model.

Points a strong answer hits:

  • Posts: 300M × 2 = 600M/day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 7K writes/s; peak ~2× ≈ 14K/s.
  • Feed reads: 300M × 10 = 3B/day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 35K reads/s; peak ≈ 70K/s.
  • Push fan-out: each post copied to ~200 follower feeds → 600M × 200 = 120B feed-writes/day ≈ 1.4M feed-writes/s.
  • That fan-out number is the argument against naive push — 200× write amplification.
  • Feed storage: cap each timeline at ~500–800 post IDs (~8 bytes each) → a few KB/user, cheap in Redis.
Model answer: 600M posts/day is ~7K writes/s, ~14K at peak. Feed opens at 10×/day give 3B reads/day, ~35K/s and ~70K/s peak. The killer number is fan-out: under pure push, each post is copied into ~200 follower timelines, so 600M posts becomes 120B feed-writes/day — about 1.4M writes/s, a 200× amplification. That's exactly why naive push doesn't survive celebrities. Feeds themselves are cheap to store — cap each per-user timeline at a few hundred post IDs at 8 bytes each, a few KB per user, easily held in Redis.

Q3 explain & apply

Design the core API for posting, following, and reading the feed — include the pagination parameters.

Points a strong answer hits:

  • POST /posts {content, mediaRefs?} → creates a post, returns postId.
  • POST /follow {targetUserId} / DELETE /follow/{targetUserId}.
  • GET /feed?cursor=<opaque>&limit=20 → returns ranked posts + next cursor.
  • Cursor is opaque (encodes last post ID/score/timestamp), not a numeric page offset.
  • Feed response hydrates post bodies + author info; supports infinite scroll.
Model answer: POST /posts with {content, mediaRefs?} creates a post; POST /follow and DELETE /follow/{id} manage the graph; and GET /feed?cursor=<opaque>&limit=20 returns a page of ranked, hydrated posts plus a nextCursor. The cursor is opaque — it encodes the last-seen post's ID/score/timestamp — rather than a numeric OFFSET, so pagination stays stable while new posts stream in at the top.

Q4 explain & apply

Give the high-level architecture and data model. What are the main services, stores, and tables?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • Services: post service, fan-out service (queue-driven), feed service, user/graph service.
  • Stores: posts DB (postId → content/author/ts), social graph store (follows), per-user feed cache (Redis lists of post IDs), object store for media.
  • Fan-out is async: a new post publishes to a queue; workers push post IDs into follower feed lists.
  • Feed read: fetch the user's cached list of post IDs → hydrate bodies from posts DB/cache → rank → return.
  • Data model: Posts{postId, authorId, content, createdAt}; Follows{followerId, followeeId}; Feed{userId → [postId...] capped}.
Model answer: Four services: a post service (writes to the Posts DB), a graph service (Follows), a queue-driven fan-out service, and a feed service. When a post is created, it's persisted and an event goes onto a queue; fan-out workers append the post ID into each follower's capped Redis timeline. On read, the feed service pulls the user's list of post IDs, hydrates bodies from the posts store/cache, ranks them, and returns a page. The model is three shapes: Posts{postId, authorId, content, createdAt}, Follows{followerId, followeeId}, and per-user Feed{userId → [postId…]} capped to a few hundred entries.

Q5 multiple choice crux

What is the core tradeoff between fan-out-on-write (push) and fan-out-on-read (pull) for feed generation?

  1. A Push gives cheap writes and expensive reads; pull gives expensive writes and cheap reads
  2. B Push precomputes each follower's feed at post time — fast reads but huge write amplification for high-follower users; pull merges followees' posts at read time — cheap writes but slow, repeated read-time work correct
  3. C They are equivalent in cost; the only difference is which database engine you use
  4. D Pull requires strong consistency while push only works with strong consistency

Why: Push (fan-out-on-write) does the work at post time, copying the post into every follower's feed — so reads are a cheap lookup of a precomputed list, but a post by a high-follower account causes millions of writes. Pull (fan-out-on-read) does nothing at write time and instead gathers and merges each followee's recent posts when the feed is requested — cheap writes, but every read repeats an expensive scatter-gather. The whole design tension is where you pay: at write time (push) or read time (pull).

Q6 explain & apply crux

This is the crux. Design the HYBRID fan-out that handles celebrity accounts. How does it work at write and at read time, and where's the threshold?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • Classify authors by follower count: normal users use push; celebrities (above a follower threshold, e.g. ~10K–1M) are NOT fanned out on write.
  • Write path: a normal user's post is pushed into follower feeds via the queue; a celebrity's post is just stored once, no fan-out.
  • Read path: load the user's precomputed (push) feed, THEN pull recent posts from the celebrities they follow, and merge/rank the two at query time.
  • Celebrity posts are cached hot (one copy read by millions) so the pull is a cheap cache hit, not N DB reads.
  • Threshold is tunable and can be per-account; avoids both million-write fan-out AND merging thousands of followees on every read.
  • Edge cases: a user who follows only celebrities gets a pure-pull feed; recompute/backfill on follow changes.
Model answer: I split authors by follower count. Normal users are pushed: their post is fanned out through the queue into each follower's precomputed timeline. Celebrities — anyone above a tunable threshold, say tens of thousands of followers — are NOT fanned out; their post is stored once. At read time I load the viewer's precomputed push feed and then pull the recent posts of the handful of celebrities they follow, merging and ranking the two sets. Because a celebrity's post is a single hot cache entry read by millions, the pull side is a cheap cache hit rather than N database reads. This gets the best of both: no million-write thundering herd on a celebrity post, and no thousand-followee scatter-gather on a normal read — with the threshold tunable per account.

Q7 multiple choice crux

For paginating an infinitely-scrolling feed where new posts constantly arrive at the top, which approach is correct?

  1. A Cursor-based pagination keyed on the last-seen post's ID/score/timestamp correct
  2. B SQL LIMIT/OFFSET, incrementing the offset per page
  3. C Re-fetch the entire feed each scroll and diff on the client
  4. D Random sampling of posts to avoid duplicates

Why: Cursor (keyset) pagination anchors the next page to the last item the client saw — its ID, score, or timestamp — so newly-inserted top-of-feed posts don't shift the pages and you never see duplicates or skips. OFFSET pagination breaks precisely here: inserting posts at the top shifts every row, so page 2 repeats or drops items. Re-fetching everything and random sampling are wasteful and don't guarantee stable ordering.

Q8 explain & apply

Discuss ranking: reverse-chronological vs. a scored feed. What signals feed a scored model, and what's the cost of moving off chrono?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • Chronological: simple, predictable, trivially mergeable across sources; but shows low-value recent posts and buries good older ones.
  • Scored: rank by a model combining recency, author affinity, predicted engagement (like/reply probability), content type, and diversity.
  • Scored feed needs feature data + a serving model at read time (or precomputed scores), adding latency and infra.
  • Ranking must still merge push + pull sources before scoring in the hybrid.
  • Tradeoff: engagement and relevance go up, but predictability, chronological trust, and simplicity go down; often a candidate-generation → ranking two-stage pipeline.
Model answer: Reverse-chronological is simple, predictable, and trivially merges push and pull sources, but it surfaces low-value recent posts and buries good older ones. A scored feed ranks by a model over recency, author affinity, predicted engagement, content type, and diversity — typically a two-stage candidate-generation then ranking pipeline. The cost is real: you need feature data and a model served at read time (or precomputed scores), which adds latency and infrastructure, and you trade away chronological predictability for engagement. In the hybrid I'd generate candidates from both the push feed and the pulled celebrity posts, then score the merged set.

Q9 explain & apply

Staff-level: a celebrity with 100M followers posts. Walk through the thundering-herd problem and how your design absorbs it, plus other failure modes at scale.

Points a strong answer hits:

  • Under naive push, one celebrity post = 100M synchronous feed-writes — a write storm that saturates the fan-out tier.
  • Hybrid avoids it entirely: the post is stored once and pulled at read time, so there's no fan-out spike.
  • The read spike (everyone loading it) is absorbed by caching the hot post once and serving millions of reads from cache/CDN.
  • Decouple any remaining fan-out through a queue with backpressure — never push synchronously; workers drain at a controlled rate.
  • Other failure modes: hot-key on the celebrity post (replicate the cache entry), feed-cache eviction (rebuild from posts DB on miss), fan-out worker lag (eventual consistency, acceptable).
  • Consistency tradeoff: feeds are eventually consistent — a post may take seconds to appear; that's fine and lets us favor availability.
Model answer: Under naive push, a 100M-follower post means 100M feed-writes in a burst — a thundering herd that melts the fan-out tier. The hybrid sidesteps it: a celebrity post is written once and pulled at read time, so there's no write fan-out at all. The correlated read spike — everyone opening the app to see it — is absorbed by caching that single hot post and serving it from cache/CDN, replicating the entry if it becomes a hot key. Any fan-out that does happen goes through a queue with backpressure so workers drain at a controlled rate instead of synchronously. The remaining failure modes — cache eviction, worker lag — are handled by rebuilding feeds from the posts DB on miss and accepting eventual consistency, since a post arriving a few seconds late is an acceptable price for availability.
