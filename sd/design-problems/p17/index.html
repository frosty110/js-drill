Design a Prescription Drug Marketplace

An online pharmacy marketplace with recurring refills, where the signature challenge is not scale but SAFETY correctness: no patient may ever be dispensed a medication that is contraindicated for them. A fail-closed clinical validation gate sits synchronously in the order-placement write path, reading a strongly-consistent view of the patient's active medication list, and re-runs on every subscription cycle because a refill that was safe at signup can become unsafe months later.

Key ideas

  • The core invariant: never dispense a medication that is contraindicated for THIS patient — no valid prescription, a drug-drug interaction, a documented allergy, duplicate therapy, or an unsafe dose.
  • This is a correctness problem wearing an e-commerce costume. Order volume is modest and predictable; do not spend the interview on sharding a catalog.
  • The safety check is a SYNCHRONOUS, FAIL-CLOSED gate in the order write path. If its dependencies are unreachable, the order is held for pharmacist review — never shipped. Post-checkout async validation is the seductively wrong answer, because a refund does not undo a patient taking the wrong drug.
  • The patient's active medication list is a first-class entity, not something derived from your own order history — the patient takes things you never dispensed, and a check blind to those is dangerously incomplete.
  • Return a LIST of severity-tiered findings, not a boolean: hard block, pharmacist review, or advisory. A single yes/no throws away exactly what the pharmacist needs to act.
  • Interaction and classification data is a licensed third-party clinical dataset you ingest and VERSION — not rules you author. Pin every decision to the rule-set version that produced it, and shadow-replay before rolling out a new one.
  • Subscriptions must re-validate every cycle: the prescription can expire or exhaust its refills, and a new medication started since signup can turn a safe refill into an interaction. The subscription stores intent, never an approval.
  • Order placement is an idempotency-keyed saga (validate → reserve → adjudicate → pay → dispense) that puts the IRREVERSIBLE step last: releasing a hold and refunding are safe compensations, un-shipping is not.
  • Read the medication list, allergies, and prescription state strongly consistent; a stale replica produces a confident false negative, which is this system's worst failure. Catalog and reviews stay eventual.
  • Compliance is functional scope: PHI encryption, least-privilege access to the medication list, an immutable append-only audit log as evidence, per-jurisdiction pharmacy licensing, and no auto-refill on controlled substances.

Q1 explain & apply

Scope an online prescription-drug marketplace with recurring refills. What are the functional requirements, and which non-functional constraint defines this problem?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • Functional: browse/search a drug catalog, submit or receive a prescription, place an order, pay (often with insurance), have a pharmacy fill and ship it, and subscribe to automatic refills.
  • The defining constraint is SAFETY correctness, not scale: the system must never dispense a medication that is contraindicated for that specific patient.
  • Validation categories: prescription required and valid, drug-drug interactions against the patient's current medication list, known allergies, duplicate therapy, dose/age/weight limits, and controlled-substance rules.
  • Regulatory constraints are first-class: PHI handling (HIPAA-style), licensed-pharmacist review, per-jurisdiction pharmacy licensing, controlled-substance restrictions on refills.
  • The system must FAIL CLOSED — if the safety check cannot run, the order does not ship. This inverts the usual availability-over-consistency default.
  • Every clinical decision needs an immutable audit trail: who or what approved this dispense, against which patient data, under which rule version.
  • Clarify: are we the pharmacy or a marketplace routing to third-party pharmacies? Do we accept e-prescriptions from prescribers, or patient-uploaded images? Which jurisdictions?
Model answer: Functionally this is an e-commerce system — search a catalog, order, pay, fulfil, and subscribe to refills — but the thing that defines it is not the commerce, it's the safety gate. A patient must never receive a medication that's contraindicated for them: no valid prescription, a dangerous interaction with something they already take, a documented allergy, a duplicate therapy, or a dose outside safe limits for their age and weight. That reframes almost every design decision. Unlike a normal storefront, this system fails CLOSED — if I can't reach the patient's medication list or the interaction database, I do not ship; I hold the order for pharmacist review. Regulation is a functional requirement, not a footnote: PHI protection, a licensed pharmacist in the loop, per-state pharmacy licensing, and tighter rules for controlled substances, which typically can't be auto-refilled at all. And every clinical decision needs an immutable audit record — which patient data, which rule version, who or what approved it. I'd clarify whether we own the pharmacy or route to third-party pharmacies, and whether prescriptions arrive electronically from prescribers or as patient uploads, because that decides how much of the verification burden is ours.

Q2 explain & apply

Estimate the scale. Why is the estimation step less interesting here than in most design problems, and what should you size instead?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • Order volume is modest by internet standards: even a large pharmacy operation is thousands of orders per minute at peak, not millions per second.
  • Traffic is predictable and diurnal — no flash-sale burst, unlike ticketing; refill batches are schedulable and can be smoothed deliberately.
  • Reads dominate (catalog browsing), and the catalog is small, highly cacheable, and rarely changes — a CDN plus a read replica covers it.
  • What actually needs sizing is the SAFETY path: a synchronous check per order against the patient's full medication list and an interaction dataset.
  • The interaction reference dataset is small enough to hold in memory (tens of MB), so the check can be single-digit milliseconds — that is what makes a synchronous in-path gate affordable.
  • Size the pharmacist review queue instead: if x% of orders are held for human review, staffing determines latency, and that's the real capacity constraint.
  • Storage is dominated by the audit log, which is append-only, must be retained for years, and is therefore a cold-storage sizing question.
Model answer: This is deliberately not a scale problem, and I'd say so rather than pad out numbers. Even a large pharmacy is doing thousands of orders per minute at peak, with predictable diurnal traffic and no flash-sale spike — refill batches are ones we schedule ourselves, so we can smooth them on purpose. Browsing dominates the read traffic but the catalog is small and nearly static, so a CDN and read replicas end that conversation. The number worth computing is the safety check: it runs synchronously on every order against the patient's medication list plus an interaction dataset that's small enough — tens of megabytes — to keep in memory. That's what makes a single-digit-millisecond in-path check affordable, and that affordability is the whole reason the architecture can be fail-closed. The real capacity constraint is human: if some percentage of orders get held for pharmacist review, pharmacist staffing sets the latency for those orders, and that queue is what I'd size carefully. Storage is dominated by the audit log — append-only, retained for years, so it's a cold-storage and retention question rather than a throughput one.

Q3 explain & apply

Sketch the core API and data model. What entities do you need, and which relationship is the one people miss?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • GET /catalog?q= — search drugs; GET /drugs/{id} — detail with dosage forms and whether a prescription is required.
  • POST /prescriptions — submit/receive an Rx; fields: patient, prescriber, drug, strength, quantity, refills_remaining, written_date, expires_at, status.
  • POST /orders with an idempotency key — returns accepted, blocked (with reasons), or held_for_review.
  • POST /subscriptions — drug, cadence, linked prescription; PATCH to pause/cancel.
  • Entities: Patient, PatientMedication (the active med list), Allergy, Prescription, Drug (catalog), Order, OrderLine, Subscription, Pharmacy, Inventory, SafetyCheck (the decision record), AuditEvent.
  • The relationship people miss: the patient's ACTIVE MEDICATION LIST is a first-class entity, separate from order history — it's what the interaction check reads, and it must include meds we didn't dispense.
  • Prescription is a stateful entity with its own lifecycle (pending → verified → active → exhausted/expired), not a field on the order.
  • SafetyCheck records the decision, the inputs it saw, and the rule-set version — so a past approval can be explained even after the rules change.
Model answer: The endpoints are unremarkable: catalog search and detail, prescription submission, order placement with an idempotency key, and subscription create/pause/cancel. Order placement returns one of three outcomes rather than a boolean — accepted, blocked with structured reasons, or held for pharmacist review — because 'we need a human to look at this' is a normal outcome here, not an error. The entities that matter are Patient, the patient's active MedicationList, Allergies, Prescription, Drug, Order, Subscription, Pharmacy, Inventory, SafetyCheck, and AuditEvent. Two modeling points I'd emphasize. First, the active medication list is its own entity, not something you derive from our order history — the patient is almost certainly taking things we never dispensed, and an interaction check that only knows about our own orders is dangerously incomplete. That's an integration problem, pulling from prescriber records or a health record system, and it's the part that makes this hard. Second, Prescription is a stateful entity with its own lifecycle — pending, verified, active, exhausted, expired — carrying refills_remaining and an expiry, not just a foreign key hanging off an order. And SafetyCheck is stored as a decision record including which rule-set version it ran under, so an approval from a year ago can still be explained after the rules have changed underneath it.

Q4 multiple choice

Where should the clinical safety validation run relative to checkout, and what happens when its dependencies are unavailable?

  1. A Client-side in the app before submitting, so the patient gets instant feedback and the backend stays simple and fast.
  2. B Asynchronously after payment, as a worker that cancels and refunds any order it later finds unsafe — this keeps the checkout path fast and highly available.
  3. C Synchronously in the order-placement write path as a fail-closed gate: if the patient's medication list or the interaction dataset is unreachable, the order is held for pharmacist review rather than shipped. correct
  4. D At the pharmacy, at pick/pack time only, since the pharmacist is the licensed professional and is legally accountable for the dispense.

Why: The check has to be an in-path gate on the write that creates a shippable order, and it has to fail closed. Client-side validation is advisory only — it can be bypassed, and it has no trustworthy access to the full medication list. Post-payment async validation is the seductively wrong answer: it optimizes availability at the cost of allowing an unsafe order to progress toward fulfilment, and a refund does not undo a patient taking the wrong drug. Pharmacist-only review is not wrong so much as insufficient — a human absolutely should be in the loop and is the accountable party, but relying on it alone gives up automated detection across the patient's entire medication list and doesn't scale. The right shape is: automated synchronous screening blocks the clear violations, escalates ambiguous ones to a pharmacist queue, and treats a dependency outage as 'hold for review', never as 'proceed'. This is the rare system where you deliberately choose consistency and safety over availability at the checkout boundary — and it's affordable precisely because the interaction dataset is small enough to check in milliseconds.

Q5 explain & apply crux

Design the safety validation engine itself. What does it check, where does the reference data come from, and how do you handle rule updates and overrides?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • Check categories: prescription required and valid (not expired, refills remaining, matches the ordered drug/strength/quantity), drug-drug interaction against the full active medication list, documented allergies and cross-sensitivities, duplicate/overlapping therapy, dose and quantity limits relative to age and weight, and controlled-substance rules.
  • Reference data (interaction and classification datasets) comes from licensed third-party clinical databases, not hand-authored rules — treat it as a versioned data dependency you ingest, not logic you own.
  • Structure it as a rules pipeline over a snapshot of patient context, so each rule is independently testable and the result is a LIST of findings, not a boolean.
  • Severity tiers drive routing: hard block, pharmacist review, or advisory warning — a single yes/no throws away the information the pharmacist needs.
  • Version the rule set and pin each SafetyCheck to the version it ran under, so decisions stay explainable and you can replay a cohort against a new version.
  • Roll out rule updates by replaying recent orders against the new version in shadow mode first — a stricter dataset can suddenly block a large population of existing subscriptions.
  • Overrides must be explicit, attributable to a named licensed pharmacist, reason-coded, and never available to the patient or to support staff.
  • Return structured, machine-readable reasons (code + severity + involved drugs) so the pharmacist UI and the patient messaging can both be built from the same result.
Model answer: I'd model it as a rules pipeline that takes an immutable snapshot of patient context — active medications, allergies, age and weight, the prescription being filled, and the drug being ordered — and returns a list of findings rather than a boolean. The categories are: is a prescription required and is this one valid, unexpired, with refills remaining and matching the drug, strength and quantity ordered; does the drug interact with anything on the active medication list; does it hit a documented allergy or a known cross-sensitivity; is it duplicate therapy with something already being taken; is the dose or quantity outside limits for this patient's age and weight; and is it a controlled substance with extra restrictions. Critically, the interaction and classification reference data is a licensed third-party clinical dataset that we ingest and version — it is emphatically not rules we author ourselves, and framing it as a versioned data dependency is the honest engineering answer. Each finding carries a severity, and severity drives routing: hard block, pharmacist review, or advisory warning. That's why a boolean is the wrong return type — the pharmacist needs to know which drugs interacted and how badly in order to act. Every SafetyCheck is pinned to the rule-set version it ran under, so a decision remains explainable after the data updates. Rolling out a new dataset version needs shadow-mode replay against recent orders first, because a stricter dataset can silently block a large slice of existing subscriptions overnight, and you want to discover that before the refills fail. Overrides exist — sometimes a prescriber genuinely intends a flagged combination — but they must be explicit, attributable to a named licensed pharmacist, reason-coded, and completely unavailable to the patient or to general support staff.

Q6 explain & apply crux

Now add the subscription/auto-refill loop. What makes a recurring medication order harder than a recurring box-of-socks order?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • The prescription itself expires and has a finite refill count — a subscription can outlive its legal authorization, so the Rx state must gate every cycle.
  • Re-validate safety on EVERY cycle, not just at signup: the patient may have started a new medication since, making a previously safe refill now interacting.
  • 'Too soon to refill' is a real check — dispensing early is both an insurance rejection and an abuse signal; compute against days-supply and last fill date.
  • Controlled substances typically cannot be auto-refilled at all; the subscription model must be able to exclude entire drug classes.
  • A blocked cycle needs a patient-and-pharmacist workflow, not a silent failure — a missed refill on a chronic medication is a clinical harm, so alert early rather than at the fill date.
  • Schedule refills to arrive BEFORE the current supply runs out, with lead time for shipping and for pharmacist review if held; work backwards from the days-supply.
  • Payment failures need retry-with-notice rather than immediate cancellation, since the consequence of a lapse is a patient going without medication.
  • Batched refill runs are a self-inflicted thundering herd — jitter them across the day rather than firing every subscription at midnight.
Model answer: The subscription is where this problem stops resembling normal e-commerce. Four things are genuinely different. First, the authorization expires: a prescription has a finite refill count and an expiry date, so a subscription can outlive the legal right to dispense — every cycle has to check prescription state, and 'renewal needed, contact your prescriber' is a first-class outcome. Second, and this is the one candidates miss, the safety check must re-run on every cycle rather than once at signup. The patient may have started a new medication two months in, and a refill that was safe in January can be an interaction in March. The subscription stores intent; it does not store an approval. Third, timing has clinical meaning in both directions: filling too early is an insurance rejection and an abuse signal, so I compute against days-supply and last-fill date, while filling too late means the patient goes without. So I schedule backwards from when the current supply runs out, leaving lead time for shipping and for a possible pharmacist review. Fourth, failure handling inverts. In normal commerce a failed payment cancels the order; here a lapse means a patient misses doses on a chronic medication, so it's retry-with-notice, escalate early, and alert both patient and pharmacist well before the fill date rather than at it. There's also a boring operational point worth making: firing every subscription at midnight is a thundering herd we inflict on ourselves and on the pharmacist queue, so I'd jitter them across the day. And controlled substances generally can't be auto-refilled at all, so the subscription model needs to exclude whole drug classes by policy.

Q7 explain & apply

Walk the order-placement write path. How do you keep it correct across the safety gate, inventory, payment, and insurance?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • Client-supplied idempotency key on POST /orders — retries from a flaky mobile network must not create duplicate medication shipments.
  • Order the steps by cost of being wrong: validate prescription and run the safety gate BEFORE reserving inventory or charging.
  • Reserve inventory at the chosen pharmacy with a TTL hold, the same reservation pattern as seat booking — but contention here is mild, so the mechanism can be simple.
  • Insurance adjudication is a slow third-party call that can partially approve or reject, changing the price — so the flow is a multi-step saga, not a single transaction.
  • Model the order as a state machine (created → validated → reserved → adjudicated → paid → filled → shipped) with explicit compensation for each backward transition.
  • Route to a pharmacy by inventory, jurisdiction/licensing, and shipping distance — a legal constraint, not just a logistics optimization.
  • Compensations must be safe: releasing a hold and refunding is fine, but 'un-shipping' is not, so the irreversible step (dispense) goes last and only after everything else has settled.
  • Write an audit event at every transition; the audit log is a product requirement, not observability.
Model answer: It's a saga, because insurance adjudication is a slow third-party call that can reject or partially approve and change the price, so there's no single transaction that spans this. I'd order the steps by the cost of being wrong: verify the prescription and run the safety gate first, before reserving anything or touching money, so the expensive and irreversible work only happens on orders that are going to be legal to fill. Then reserve inventory at a chosen pharmacy with a TTL hold — the same reservation pattern as seat booking, though contention is mild here so it doesn't need the elaborate machinery. Pharmacy routing is constrained by licensing and jurisdiction before it's optimized for distance; that's a legal filter, not a logistics preference. Then adjudicate insurance, then charge the patient responsibility, then dispense and ship. The order is a state machine with explicit compensation for each backward transition, and the key discipline is that the irreversible step — actually dispensing — is last, after everything reversible has settled. Releasing a hold and issuing a refund are safe compensations; un-shipping a controlled substance is not. The whole path is idempotency-keyed, because a retry from a flaky phone connection must never turn into two shipments of the same medication. And every transition writes an audit event — here that's a product and compliance requirement, not just observability.

Q8 multiple choice

Which part of this system most clearly requires strong consistency, and which is safely eventual?

  1. A The patient's active medication list and allergy record read by the safety gate must be strongly consistent; catalog search results and product reviews can be eventual. correct
  2. B The catalog must be strongly consistent so prices never differ between search and detail; the medication list can be eventual because a pharmacist reviews every order anyway.
  3. C Everything must be strongly consistent, since this is a regulated healthcare system and eventual consistency is not permitted under HIPAA.
  4. D Order status must be strongly consistent for the customer-facing UI; the medication list can lag because interactions are rare enough to accept the risk.

Why: The safety gate is only as good as the data it reads. If a medication the patient started yesterday hasn't propagated to the replica the interaction check queries, the check confidently returns 'safe' against a stale picture — a silent false negative, which is the worst failure this system has. So the medication list, allergy record, and prescription state are read strongly consistent (primary or read-your-writes) at the moment of the check. Catalog search, product descriptions, and reviews are classic eventual-consistency territory: a stale price or a missing new listing is a minor annoyance. The 'everything strongly consistent' option confuses regulation with consistency models — HIPAA governs privacy, access control and auditability, and says nothing about replication semantics. And accepting a lagging medication list because 'interactions are rare' inverts the whole design: rare-but-catastrophic is precisely the profile that justifies paying for strong consistency on a narrow, low-volume path.

Q9 explain & apply

What are the main tradeoffs and failure modes, including the compliance ones? What would you cut if the interview were half over?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • Fail-closed costs availability: an interaction-database outage degrades into a pharmacist queue, which means slower orders — accepted deliberately, and worth stating as a choice.
  • False positives have a real cost too: over-blocking sends patients elsewhere and buries pharmacists in review, so severity tiering is what keeps the system usable.
  • PHI everywhere: encrypt at rest and in transit, minimize which services see the medication list, strict least-privilege access, and log every access — support staff must not be able to browse patient medications.
  • The audit log is immutable and append-only, retained for years; it's evidence, so it cannot be mutable application state.
  • A rule-set update can retroactively block a large population of active subscriptions — needs shadow replay and a staged rollout.
  • Jurisdiction: pharmacy licensing is per-state/country, so routing and even catalog visibility are location-dependent.
  • Controlled substances need tighter handling: no auto-refill, stricter identity verification, quantity limits, and reporting obligations.
  • If short on time, cut catalog search, reviews, and the recommendation surface — keep prescription verification, the safety gate, the order saga, and the subscription re-validation loop, because that's the whole problem.
Model answer: The central tradeoff is one I'd make explicitly rather than let the interviewer find: we choose consistency and safety over availability at the checkout boundary, so an interaction-database outage degrades into a pharmacist review queue and slower orders rather than into unsafe shipments. That's the right call, but it isn't free, and I'd name the cost. The mirror risk is over-blocking — false positives push patients to competitors and drown pharmacists in review volume, which is why severity tiering rather than a single block/allow verdict is what keeps the system usable in practice. On compliance: PHI is everywhere, so it's encrypted in transit and at rest, the medication list is visible to as few services as possible, access is least-privilege and logged, and support staff cannot browse patient medications. The audit log is append-only and immutable because it's evidence, not application state. Two operational hazards worth flagging: a clinical dataset update can retroactively block a large population of active subscriptions, so it needs shadow replay and staged rollout; and pharmacy licensing is per-jurisdiction, so routing and sometimes even catalog visibility are location-dependent. Controlled substances are their own regime — no auto-refill, stricter identity checks, quantity limits, reporting. If I were short on time I'd drop catalog search, reviews and recommendations without hesitation and spend everything on prescription verification, the fail-closed safety gate, the order saga, and per-cycle subscription re-validation. Those four are the problem; the storefront around them is the easy part.
