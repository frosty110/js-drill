Design a Human Review Queue

Automated systems escalate what they should not decide: flagged content, probable sanctions matches, disputed charges, low-confidence extractions. Design the layer that distributes that work to human reviewers. The signature challenge is that the workers are people, so the queue has to survive a reviewer closing their laptop mid-item, and throughput is set by staffing rather than by capacity you can provision.

Components in play 7

  • Work Queue signature — The workers are people, so the queue hands out leases rather than assignments and reclaims anything a reviewer abandons without needing the client to cooperate.
  • Message Ordering signature — Arrival order carries no information about importance, so the queue orders by risk and deadline with an aging term that bounds how long the tail can wait.
  • Reservation / Hold signature — A claimed item is held under an expiring lease, which gives at-most-one-active-reviewer without a lock service and returns the item automatically when someone closes their laptop.
  • Audit Log & Provenance signature — A reviewer's decision is written as an immutable event with its reason and the lease that authorised it, and a correction references the original rather than overwriting it.
  • Agent Decision Record signature — The reviewer's verdict is stored beside the agent's recommendation rather than replacing it, so the disagreement rate is a number instead of an impression.
  • Rate Limiting — Admission into the queue is metered per lane, so a 500K-item batch dump drains gradually instead of displacing the live stream that has deadlines attached.
  • Tenant Isolation — Reviewer pools and queues are scoped per tenant, since someone cleared to see one institution's data usually is not cleared for another's.

The brief

Functional requirements

  • Accept items escalated by upstream automated systems, hold them until a qualified human decides, and emit the decision back.
  • Distribute work so no two reviewers hold the same item and no item is stranded when a reviewer disappears.
  • Order by risk and by external deadline, with skill and permission routing, and dual control where policy demands it.
  • Record every decision with a reason as an immutable event, and expose queue health to the people who staff it.
  • Out of scope: the upstream classifiers that generate escalations, the reviewer UI itself, and the downstream action taken on the decision.

Scale & constraints

  • ~200 reviewers across 3 time zones, each clearing 40-80 items/hour, so peak service rate is ~4 items/sec across the fleet
  • Arrival is spiky and machine-driven: a steady ~2/sec baseline with 500K-item batch dumps when an upstream model or list is re-run
  • That gap is the design problem: a single batch can hold 70 reviewer-days of work and arrives in minutes
  • Queue depth is therefore measured in hours of backlog, not in rows; 500K items is a number, 70 days is a decision
  • Per-item state is small (a few KB of references), so this is a coordination and ordering problem, not a storage one

Key ideas

  • Work is claimed under a lease, never assigned. A reviewer who walks away must return their item to the queue automatically, because they will not release it themselves.
  • The lease is the whole concurrency story: it gives you at-most-one-active-reviewer per item without distributed locks and without an item ever being stranded.
  • FIFO is wrong. Order by risk and by the external deadline, because upstream systems dump batches and arrival order carries no information about importance.
  • Priority without an aging term starves the tail forever. Aging is not a nicety; it is what stops a permanent backlog of low-priority items nobody ever sees.
  • Rate-limit what enters the queue, not what the matcher produces. Humans are the bottleneck, and a flooded queue means the urgent items sit behind noise.
  • The reviewer's decision is a first-class event with a required reason, because it is simultaneously an audit fact, a training label, and the only real quality signal you have.
  • Queue depth is a staffing equation, not a scaling one. Little's law tells you the wait, and no amount of infrastructure fixes an arrival rate above the service rate.
  • Dual control and skill routing belong in the data model. A policy that says two people must sign is a schema constraint, not a process document.

Q1 explain & apply crux

Scope a human review queue. What makes it different from an ordinary work queue?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • Functional: accept escalations from upstream systems, hold them until a qualified human decides, emit the decision, and never lose or duplicate an item.
  • The workers are people, which changes the failure model completely: they take breaks, close laptops, go to lunch mid-item, and leave the company.
  • So work is claimed with a lease that expires, rather than assigned to a worker who is trusted to finish or explicitly fail.
  • Service rate is set by headcount and is essentially fixed in the short term. You cannot autoscale reviewers, which makes backpressure a product decision rather than an infrastructure one.
  • Arrival is machine-driven and spiky while service is human and steady, so the two sides are mismatched by orders of magnitude.
  • Items usually carry an external deadline — a regulatory clock, an SLA, a payment authorization window — so ordering is not free.
  • Not every reviewer can see every item: skill, language, seniority and permission all constrain routing, so this is a matching problem rather than a pop from a single queue.
  • Out of scope: the upstream classifiers, the reviewer UI, and whatever downstream action the decision triggers.
Model answer: It takes escalations from upstream automated systems, holds them until a qualified human decides, and emits that decision without ever losing or duplicating an item. What makes it different from an ordinary work queue is that the workers are people, so the failure model is not process crashes, it is someone closing a laptop mid-item and going home. That means work has to be claimed under an expiring lease rather than assigned, because a human will never reliably release it. It also means the service rate is headcount and is fixed in the short term — you cannot autoscale reviewers — while arrival is machine-driven and spiky, so the two sides are mismatched by orders of magnitude and backpressure becomes a product decision rather than an infrastructure one. And routing is a matching problem, not a pop, because skill, language, seniority and permission all constrain who may see what.

Q2 explain & apply crux

Estimate the scale and identify which number actually constrains the system.

Points a strong answer hits:

  • 200 reviewers, each clearing 40-80 items/hour, so roughly 12K items/hour at full staffing, about 4/sec across the fleet at peak.
  • Across three time zones with realistic coverage, sustained daily capacity is maybe 100-150K items/day, not the theoretical maximum.
  • Arrival baseline is around 2/sec from steady escalation, which fits comfortably.
  • The problem is the batch: re-running an upstream model or ingesting a list delta can dump 500K items in minutes.
  • 500K items at 12K/hour is roughly 42 hours of wall clock at full staffing, or about 70 reviewer-days of work, arriving instantly.
  • That is the number that matters, and it should be expressed in time rather than rows — 500K is a row count, 42 hours is a decision.
  • Storage is trivial: a few KB of references per item, so even a million queued items is single-digit gigabytes.
  • So this is a coordination, ordering and backpressure problem. Nothing here is bound by CPU, storage, or database throughput.
Model answer: Two hundred reviewers clearing forty to eighty items an hour gives about twelve thousand an hour at full staffing, call it four a second across the fleet at peak, and realistically a hundred to a hundred and fifty thousand a day once you account for actual coverage across time zones. Steady arrival of around two a second fits fine. The constraint is the batch: re-running an upstream model can dump five hundred thousand items in minutes, which is roughly forty-two hours of wall clock or seventy reviewer-days of work arriving instantly. I would deliberately express backlog in hours rather than rows, because five hundred thousand is a row count while forty-two hours is something a manager can make a decision about. Storage is negligible — a few kilobytes per item — so nothing here is bound by CPU or the database. It is entirely a coordination and backpressure problem.

Q3 explain & apply crux

Deep dive: design the claim mechanism. How does a reviewer get an item, and what happens when they abandon it?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • A reviewer requests work; the system atomically selects the highest-priority eligible item and marks it leased to that reviewer with an expiry, then returns it.
  • In Postgres this is a single statement: UPDATE ... SET leased_by, lease_expires_at WHERE id = (SELECT ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED LIMIT 1) RETURNING *.
  • SKIP LOCKED is what makes concurrent claims work without contention — each requester walks past rows another transaction is already taking rather than blocking behind them.
  • The lease has a TTL sized to the realistic work time (say 30 minutes for a complex case), not to a request timeout.
  • A sweeper reclaims expired leases and returns items to the pool. This is the mechanism that handles the closed laptop, and it needs no cooperation from the client.
  • Heartbeats from an open review session extend the lease, so a genuinely slow reviewer is not interrupted mid-item while an absent one is reclaimed promptly.
  • Track reclaim count per item: an item reclaimed repeatedly is either too hard, mis-routed, or broken, and should be escalated rather than recycled forever.
  • Do not use a distributed lock service for this. The lease column in the database the items already live in is simpler, survives restarts, and has no split-brain story.
  • Idempotency on submit: a reviewer double-submitting, or submitting after their lease expired and the item was reclaimed, must not produce two decisions. Check the lease is still theirs inside the write.
Model answer: A reviewer asks for work and the system atomically picks the highest-priority eligible item, stamps it with their id and a lease expiry, and returns it. In Postgres that is a single update selecting FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED, which is what lets two hundred reviewers claim concurrently without contending — each one walks past rows another transaction is already taking. The lease TTL is sized to realistic work time rather than to a request timeout, maybe thirty minutes, and an open review session heartbeats to extend it so a slow reviewer is not interrupted while an absent one is reclaimed. A sweeper returns expired leases to the pool, and that is the entire answer to the closed laptop: it needs no cooperation from the client. I would count reclaims per item, because one that keeps getting abandoned is too hard or mis-routed rather than unlucky. And I would not reach for a distributed lock service — a lease column in the database the items already live in is simpler and has no split-brain story.

Q4 multiple choice crux

You order the queue strictly by priority score. After a month, a large set of low-priority items has never been reviewed. What is the fix?

  1. A Increase reviewer headcount until the low-priority tail is reachable
  2. B Add an aging term so an item's effective priority rises with time in queue, which bounds worst-case wait instead of leaving it unbounded correct
  3. C Process the queue in FIFO order instead, so every item is eventually reached
  4. D Expire low-priority items after 30 days, since nobody needed them

Why: Strict priority ordering gives unbounded wait to anything below the arrival rate of higher-priority work — the tail is not slow, it is never reached, and adding headcount only moves the line at which starvation begins. Aging fixes the class: effective priority becomes a function of both risk and time waiting, so every item eventually rises to the top and worst-case wait is bounded by a number you choose. Plain FIFO overcorrects and is worse in this domain, because arrival order carries no information about importance and an urgent item would then queue behind a batch dump. Expiring items is occasionally a legitimate product decision but it is a different one, and doing it silently to manage queue depth means quietly deciding never to review a category of work — which, in a compliance or moderation context, is exactly the thing an auditor is looking for.

Q5 explain & apply crux

Deep dive: how do you order the queue, and how do you keep an upstream batch from burying the urgent work?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • Effective priority combines risk score, time-to-deadline, and an aging term, recomputed cheaply rather than stored as a frozen value.
  • Time-to-deadline matters more than risk as the deadline approaches, so the ordering function should let deadline dominate late even for a low-risk item.
  • Partition the queue by routing dimension (skill, language, permission, tenant) so a claim only ever scans an eligible subset — this is both a correctness and an index-performance concern.
  • Rate-limit admission into the queue rather than upstream production. The classifier is cheap and the humans are the bottleneck, so throttle at the boundary where the scarce resource is.
  • Give batch-originated work its own admission lane with a lower fill rate, so a 500K dump drains gradually instead of displacing the live stream.
  • Reserve capacity for the urgent lane: a fixed fraction of claims always come from the deadline-driven pool, so a backlog can never fully crowd it out.
  • Surface backlog in hours, not items, and make it the headline operational metric because it is the one that maps to a staffing decision.
  • When arrival exceeds service for long enough, the honest answers are: add reviewers, raise the auto-decision threshold upstream, or explicitly stop accepting a category. Silently growing the queue is not one of them.
  • Index carefully: a queue ordered by a computed priority over a partitioned, filtered set is the query that will be run millions of times, and it is where this design either works or falls over.
Model answer: Effective priority is risk, time-to-deadline and an aging term combined, with deadline dominating as it approaches so a low-risk item that is about to breach still gets pulled forward. I would partition by routing dimension — skill, language, permission, tenant — so a claim only scans an eligible subset, which is both correctness and index performance. For the batch problem, the key move is rate-limiting admission into the queue rather than upstream production: the classifier is cheap, the humans are scarce, so you throttle at the boundary where the scarce resource actually is. Batch-originated work gets its own lane with a lower fill rate, and I would reserve a fixed fraction of claims for the deadline-driven pool so a backlog can never fully crowd out urgent work. The operational metric I would put front and centre is backlog in hours rather than items, because that is the number that maps to a staffing decision. And when arrival genuinely exceeds service, the honest options are more reviewers, a higher upstream auto-decide threshold, or explicitly declining a category — quietly letting the queue grow is not one of them.

Q6 multiple choice crux

A reviewer's lease expires while they are still working. They submit their decision 30 seconds later, but the item was already reclaimed and given to someone else. What should happen?

  1. A Accept the decision, since the reviewer did the work in good faith
  2. B Reject the write because the lease is no longer theirs, tell them the item was reassigned, and let the current holder decide — the lease check belongs inside the write transaction correct
  3. C Accept whichever decision arrives first and discard the second
  4. D Merge both decisions and escalate if they disagree

Why: The lease is only meaningful if it is checked at write time, in the same transaction as the write. Accepting a decision from an expired lease means two reviewers can be working the same item and both can commit, so the item's outcome depends on ordering rather than on policy — and in an audited context you now have a decision attributed to someone who did not hold the item. First-write-wins has the same defect and additionally makes the loser's work silently vanish. Merging and escalating on disagreement sounds thorough but invents a dual-control workflow the policy did not ask for, and it triggers on an accident rather than on a rule. The right behaviour is to reject with a clear message, which is also the signal that the lease TTL is too short for this class of item — a rising rate of these is a tuning input, not just an error.

Q7 explain & apply crux

What is recorded when a reviewer decides, and why does that record matter so much?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • The decision is an immutable event, not a mutation of the item's row: who decided, what they decided, the required reason, the lease they held, the version of the guidance they were shown, and the time taken.
  • The reason is mandatory and structured rather than free text, because it is the field every downstream consumer actually needs.
  • It is an audit fact: in a regulated context, who decided and on what basis is the record that has to survive.
  • It is a training label: reviewer decisions are the highest-quality supervision the upstream automation will ever get, and they are being generated anyway.
  • It is a quality signal: agreement between reviewers on the same item measures both reviewer calibration and item ambiguity.
  • Deliberately double-assign a small random sample to two reviewers to measure inter-rater agreement, which is the only way to know whether the guidance is clear.
  • Time-per-item by category feeds capacity planning and finds guidance that is confusing rather than work that is hard.
  • Dual control where policy requires it: two independent decisions, the second reviewer blind to the first, modeled as two events plus a resolution rather than as an approval flag.
  • Never let a decision be silently overwritten. A correction is a new event referencing the old one, so the history of a reversal is preserved.
Model answer: The decision is an immutable event rather than an update to the item: who decided, what, the mandatory structured reason, the lease they held, the guidance version they were shown, and how long it took. That record does three jobs at once, which is why it deserves a schema rather than a comment field. It is the audit fact that has to survive in a regulated setting. It is the highest-quality training label the upstream automation will ever get, and it is being produced anyway as a by-product of work you are already paying for. And it is the quality signal — I would deliberately double-assign a small random sample to two reviewers, blind, to measure inter-rater agreement, because that is the only honest way to tell whether the guidance is clear or the item is genuinely ambiguous. Where policy requires dual control I would model it as two independent blind decisions plus a resolution, not an approval flag. And a correction is always a new event referencing the old one, never an overwrite.

Q8 explain & apply crux

How do you make this observable to the people who have to staff it?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • The headline number is backlog in hours at current staffing, not queue depth in items, because it is the one that translates into a decision.
  • Project time-to-drain from arrival rate and service rate, and alert when projected drain exceeds the nearest deadline in the queue.
  • Deadline breach risk is the real SLO: how many items will miss their external deadline at current throughput, not how many are waiting.
  • Per-lane depth, since an aggregate number hides a starved skill lane where one language or permission group has no available reviewers.
  • Reclaim rate: rising reclaims mean items are too hard, mis-routed, or the lease TTL is wrong.
  • Reviewer throughput distribution rather than the average, because the average hides both the person who is struggling and the person clicking through without reading.
  • Inter-rater agreement trend, which detects guidance drift before it shows up as bad decisions downstream.
  • The dangerous silent failure is a lane with no eligible reviewers: items enter, nothing errors, and they simply wait forever while the aggregate dashboard looks healthy. Alert on oldest-item-age per lane, not globally.
  • Overturn rate from any downstream appeal or QA process, which is the closest thing to a ground-truth signal on decision quality.
Model answer: The headline should be backlog in hours at current staffing rather than depth in items, and beside it a projected time to drain, with an alert when the projection runs past the nearest deadline sitting in the queue. The real SLO is deadline-breach risk — how many items will miss their external clock at current throughput — because that is the thing with consequences. I would break every depth metric out per routing lane, since an aggregate hides a starved lane where one language or permission group has no available reviewers. The silent failure I would specifically alert on is oldest-item-age per lane: a lane with no eligible reviewers accepts items, throws no errors, and lets them wait forever while the global dashboard looks fine. Beyond that: reclaim rate as a signal that items are too hard or the lease is too short, reviewer throughput as a distribution rather than an average, and inter-rater agreement as an early warning that the guidance has drifted.

Q9 explain & apply crux

Staff-level wrap-up: what breaks, and what would you refuse to build?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • The failure that actually hurts is sustained arrival above service rate. No infrastructure fixes it, and the queue growing is a symptom of a product decision nobody made.
  • Little's law is the framing to bring: average wait equals queue length divided by throughput, so a stable queue length is the only sustainable state and everything else is a countdown.
  • The design must therefore make backpressure visible upstream: the escalation threshold on the automated system is the real throttle, and it belongs to whoever owns that threshold.
  • Multi-region and time zones: follow-the-sun routing means an item claimed in one region and abandoned must be reclaimable in another, so lease state needs a clear ownership model.
  • Do not build a general workflow engine. The temptation is to generalize into arbitrary multi-step human workflows, and the result is a product with no opinion that is harder to reason about than the queue it replaced.
  • Do not let reviewers pick their own items. Self-selection means the easy items go first, average handling time looks great, and the hard ones age forever.
  • Do not silently expire items to manage depth. Deciding not to review a category is a legitimate product decision and an illegitimate infrastructure one.
  • Watch for gaming: any per-reviewer throughput metric will be optimized against, so pair it with agreement and overturn rate or you will get fast, confident, wrong decisions.
  • The natural evolution is feeding decisions back to raise the upstream auto-decide threshold, which shrinks the queue at the source — the queue's long-term job is to make itself smaller.
Model answer: The failure that actually matters is sustained arrival above service rate, and no amount of infrastructure fixes it — Little's law says a stable queue length is the only sustainable state, so a growing queue is a countdown to a product decision nobody has made yet. So the design's real job is to make that visible upstream, because the escalation threshold on the automated system is the true throttle. Two things I would refuse to build: a general workflow engine, because generalizing into arbitrary human workflows produces something with no opinion that is harder to reason about than the queue it replaced; and reviewer self-selection of items, because the easy ones go first, handling time looks wonderful, and the hard ones age forever. I would also be careful with per-reviewer throughput metrics, since anything measured gets optimized against and the failure mode is fast, confident, wrong decisions — so pair it with agreement and overturn rate. And the long-term goal is that decisions feed back to raise the upstream auto-decide threshold, because the queue's job is ultimately to make itself smaller.
