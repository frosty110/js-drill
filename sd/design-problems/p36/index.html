Design a Business Onboarding & Document Verification Pipeline

A business signs up: incorporation documents, ownership declarations, IDs for the people behind it. Verify the claims well enough to let them transact. The signature challenge is that extracting what a document says and establishing that it is true are two different problems, and the second one requires walking an ownership graph until you reach human beings.

Canonical Design Problems 9 questions

Results carried by this link

Components in play 4

  • Object / Blob Storage signature — The uploaded document is evidence with the same retention obligation as the decision, so the original is content-addressed and never replaced by anything derived from it.
  • Work Queue signature — Verification hops have wildly different latencies, from a 2-second API call to a manual check taking days, so the work is queued per hop rather than held open per application.
  • Idempotency signature — A vendor timeout is ambiguous about whether the work happened, so the retry has to record one result and incur one charge no matter how many attempts it took.
  • Conflict Resolution signature — The document, the registry and the applicant will state different addresses, so precedence is declared in advance and the winning source is recorded rather than silently overwriting.

The brief

Functional requirements

  • Accept documents and declared business details, extract structured fields, verify them against authoritative sources, and return an onboarding decision.
  • Resolve the ownership structure to natural persons, then screen and verify each of them.
  • Route low-confidence extractions and source conflicts to a human, and support re-submission of a rejected or missing document.
  • Produce an auditable record: what was claimed, what each source said, which source won, and why the decision went the way it did.
  • Out of scope: sanctions screening itself (a separate system this one calls), the reviewer UI, and the downstream account provisioning.

Scale & constraints

  • ~5K business applications/day, each with 3-8 documents, so ~25K documents/day, about 0.3/sec — low throughput, long tail
  • Extraction is seconds; verification is bounded by third parties, from 2s for an API registry lookup to several DAYS for a manual or postal check
  • So a case's wall-clock life is minutes at p50 and days at p99, which is a state-machine problem rather than a latency one
  • Documents are 200KB-20MB scans; 25K/day at 2MB averages ~50GB/day into object storage, retained 5-7 years
  • Vendor calls cost $0.50-$5.00 each and a full ownership walk can trigger 10-40 of them, so cost per application is the constraint that bites

Key ideas

  • Extraction and verification are separate stages with separate failure modes. Extraction says what the document claims; verification says whether the claim is true, and conflating them is the classic mistake on this problem.
  • The uploaded document is evidence with the same retention obligation as the decision it supports. Content-address it, never mutate it, and keep the original alongside anything derived from it.
  • Per-field confidence, not per-document. A registration number read at 0.99 and a director name read at 0.62 need different handling, and one document-level score throws that away.
  • Sources will disagree. Declare precedence up front and record which source won, because 'the registry and the document differ' is the normal case rather than the exception.
  • Ownership is a graph, not a field. Walk it until you reach natural persons, with a depth limit and cycle detection, because circular ownership structures exist and some are deliberate.
  • The pipeline is asynchronous with a tail measured in days, so the application's state machine must be able to wait on a human at a third party without holding anything open.
  • Make every stage idempotent and keyed on the content hash, because retries and re-uploads of the same file are routine and re-verification costs real money per call.
  • Partial verification is a first-class outcome. Most businesses clear most checks, and the product decision is which combination of passes is enough to transact.

Q1 explain & apply crux

Scope a business onboarding and document verification pipeline. What are the stages, and which distinction matters most?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • Functional: accept documents and declared details, extract structured fields, verify them against authoritative sources, resolve ownership to natural persons, and return a decision.
  • The distinction that matters most is extraction versus verification. Extraction tells you what the document says; verification tells you whether it is true.
  • A perfectly extracted field from a forged or outdated document is worthless, and treating a high OCR confidence as evidence of truth is the classic failure on this problem.
  • So the pipeline has at least four stages: intake and storage, extraction, verification against sources, and adjudication.
  • Ownership resolution is its own stage and is recursive: a company owned by a company owned by a trust, walked until you reach human beings.
  • Non-functional: the tail is measured in days because some verifications are manual at the other end, so this is a long-running state machine.
  • Auditability: the record must show what was claimed, what each source said, which source won, and why. This decision gets examined later.
  • Out of scope: sanctions screening itself is a separate system this one calls, and so is downstream account provisioning.
Model answer: Take documents and declared details, extract structured fields, verify them against authoritative sources, resolve the ownership structure down to real people, and return a decision with an auditable record behind it. The distinction I would put on the board first is extraction versus verification, because they get conflated constantly: extraction tells you what the document says, verification tells you whether that is true, and a perfectly extracted field from a forged or expired document is worth nothing. High OCR confidence is not evidence. Ownership resolution is its own recursive stage, since a company owned by a company owned by a trust has to be walked until you reach human beings. And the shape of the whole thing is a long-running state machine rather than a request path, because some verifications are manual at the far end and take days.

Q2 explain & apply crux

Estimate the load, and say which dimension actually constrains the design.

Points a strong answer hits:

  • 5K business applications per day, each carrying 3-8 documents, so roughly 25K documents/day — about 0.3/sec. Throughput is not the problem.
  • Extraction is seconds per document, easily parallelized, and cheap relative to everything else.
  • Verification latency is set entirely by third parties: 2 seconds for an API-backed registry, minutes for a slower one, and days where the check is manual or postal at the other end.
  • So p50 wall clock is minutes and p99 is days. That spread is the design driver: you cannot hold a request open, and you cannot poll a database row for three days without a real state machine.
  • Storage: 25K documents/day at ~2MB average is ~50GB/day, retained 5-7 years, so tens of terabytes. Object storage, cheap, but the retention obligation is real.
  • Cost: vendor calls run $0.50-$5.00 each, and a full ownership walk on a complex structure can trigger 10-40 of them.
  • That puts cost per application in the tens of dollars, which at 5K/day is a five-to-six-figure monthly line item — the number that gets attention.
  • So the constraints are cost per application and tail latency, not QPS or storage. Design for caching verification results and for waiting cheaply.
Model answer: Five thousand applications a day at three to eight documents each is about twenty-five thousand documents, roughly a third of a document per second, so throughput is a non-issue. Extraction is seconds and cheap. What dominates is the verification tail: an API-backed registry answers in seconds, but some checks are manual at the far end and take days, so p50 is minutes and p99 is days. That spread is the design driver, because you cannot hold a request open and you should not be polling a row for three days without a real state machine. Storage is fifty gigabytes a day retained for five to seven years, which is tens of terabytes of object storage — cheap, but the retention is a genuine obligation. The number that actually bites is cost: vendor calls are fifty cents to five dollars and a full ownership walk can fire ten to forty of them, so cost per application lands in the tens of dollars and that is what I would design around.

Q3 explain & apply crux

Deep dive: walk through what happens to a single uploaded document.

Points a strong answer hits:

  • Accept the upload, scan it for malware, and store the original immediately, content-addressed by hash, before anything parses it.
  • The original is evidence: it carries the same retention obligation as the decision it supports and must never be mutated or replaced by a derived version.
  • Content-addressing gives deduplication for free — the same articles of incorporation uploaded twice is one artifact and one extraction, which matters because extraction and verification cost money.
  • Classify the document type first (incorporation certificate, utility bill, passport, ownership declaration), because the extraction schema depends on it and a misclassification produces confidently wrong fields.
  • Extract into a typed schema with per-field confidence, not one document-level score. A registration number at 0.99 and a director name at 0.62 need different handling.
  • Route any field below its threshold to human review rather than propagating a guess into a verification call or a decision.
  • Thresholds are per-field, because the cost of being wrong differs: a misread company number fails a lookup loudly, a misread director name fails a screening silently.
  • Check document validity separately from content: expiry dates, issuing authority, and tamper signals. A valid extraction from an expired document is still a rejection.
  • Emit an extraction event referencing the artifact hash and the extractor version, so a later extractor improvement can be replayed against the original.
Model answer: Scan it, then store the original immediately, content-addressed by hash, before anything touches it — the original is evidence and carries the same retention obligation as the decision. Content-addressing also gives deduplication for free, which matters more than it sounds because the same document uploaded twice would otherwise cost a second extraction and a second round of paid verification. Then classify the document type, because the extraction schema depends on it and a misclassification produces fields that are confidently wrong. Extraction produces a typed schema with per-field confidence rather than one document-level number, and anything below its threshold routes to a human instead of propagating a guess into a paid lookup. I would set those thresholds per field, because a misread company number fails loudly at the registry while a misread director name fails silently at screening. And validity is checked separately from content: a perfectly extracted expired document is still a rejection.

Q4 multiple choice crux

The uploaded incorporation certificate says the registered address is on Oak Street. The government registry API says Elm Street. What should the system do?

  1. A Trust the government registry, since it is the authoritative source, and overwrite the extracted value
  2. B Record both, apply a declared precedence rule, and surface the conflict — with which source won and why stored on the decision, because source disagreement is the normal case and the resolution has to be auditable correct
  3. C Trust the document, since the applicant submitted it as their own declaration
  4. D Reject the application for inconsistent information

Why: Sources disagreeing is routine, not exceptional — registries lag real filings, documents go stale, and applicants move. So the design question is not which source is right in general but whether the resolution rule was declared in advance and recorded afterwards. Silently overwriting with the registry value loses the fact that the applicant told you something different, which is exactly what you would want to see later if this turned out to be fraud. Trusting the document inverts the usual precedence and makes self-declaration authoritative. Rejecting outright is far too blunt for the single most common discrepancy in onboarding, and it would fail a large share of legitimate businesses. The auditable answer keeps both values, applies a stated precedence, and records which won.

Q5 explain & apply crux

Deep dive: resolve the ownership structure. How do you find the humans behind a company?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • Beneficial ownership is a graph problem, not a field. A company is owned by companies, trusts and people, and the obligation is to reach natural persons above a threshold, typically 25%.
  • Walk it recursively: for each corporate owner, look up its own ownership from the relevant registry and recurse.
  • Multiply proportional stakes down the chain — 50% of a company that owns 60% of the applicant is a 30% effective stake, which crosses a 25% threshold that neither hop does alone.
  • Hard depth limit, because a chain can be arbitrarily long and each hop is a paid lookup. Exceeding it is an escalation, not a failure.
  • Cycle detection is mandatory: circular ownership exists, sometimes accidentally and sometimes deliberately to frustrate exactly this walk.
  • Jurisdiction changes everything mid-walk. Some registries are free APIs, some are paid, some are manual requests taking days, and some jurisdictions publish nothing at all.
  • So the walk is a long-running workflow with heterogeneous per-hop latency, not a synchronous recursive function.
  • An opaque hop is a finding rather than an error — a chain that disappears into a jurisdiction with no public registry is itself a risk signal and should be surfaced as one.
  • Each natural person found then needs identity verification and screening, so the walk fans out into more work proportional to structure complexity.
  • Cache aggressively by entity with a freshness policy: the same parent company appears across many applications and re-walking it every time is pure cost.
Model answer: Ownership is a graph, so the walk is recursive: for each corporate owner, look up its own ownership and recurse until you reach natural persons above the threshold, usually twenty-five percent. The part people miss is that stakes multiply down the chain — fifty percent of a company holding sixty percent of the applicant is an effective thirty percent, which crosses a threshold that neither hop crosses alone. It needs a hard depth limit and mandatory cycle detection, because circular ownership genuinely exists and is sometimes deliberately constructed to frustrate this exact walk. Each hop can be a free API, a paid lookup, or a manual request taking days depending on jurisdiction, so it is a long-running workflow rather than a recursive function. An opaque hop into a jurisdiction that publishes nothing is a finding, not an error, and I would surface it as a risk signal. And I would cache by entity, because the same parent company shows up across many applications and re-walking it is pure cost.

Q6 multiple choice crux

A verification vendor times out. Your workflow retries and the call succeeds on the second attempt. What must be true so this does not corrupt the case or the bill?

  1. A The retry must use a different vendor to avoid the same timeout
  2. B The verification step must be idempotent, keyed so a repeat produces one recorded result and, where the vendor supports it, one charge correct
  3. C The workflow must roll back the whole case and start the application over
  4. D The first attempt's result must be discarded because a timeout means it failed

Why: A timeout is ambiguous by nature: the vendor may have completed the work and lost the response, so the naive retry produces two verifications, potentially two conflicting recorded results, and two charges. The fix is the standard one — an idempotency key on the call, plus a keyed write so the same logical verification records once no matter how many attempts it took. Failing over to a different vendor changes the answer source mid-case and makes the audit record harder to defend, quite apart from not solving duplication. Rolling back the whole case throws away expensive completed work for a transient network event. And assuming the first attempt failed is precisely the wrong inference from a timeout: you do not know that it failed, only that you did not hear back.

Q7 explain & apply crux

How is the case orchestrated when parts of it take days?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • A durable workflow per application: the workflow is deterministic and replayable, and every side-effecting call is a retryable activity.
  • Waiting must be free. A case blocked for three days on a manual registry check should cost a row, not a thread or an open connection.
  • Model the case as an explicit state machine — received, extracting, verifying, awaiting-applicant, awaiting-third-party, in-review, decided — because 'pending' as a single state hides every operationally different situation.
  • Fan out independent verifications in parallel and join, since most are unrelated: the registry lookup does not depend on the ID check.
  • Partial completion is the normal state. Most applications clear most checks, so the model must express 'verified except for X' rather than a global pass or fail.
  • That makes conditional approval a real product option: allow limited transacting while a slow check completes, with a bounded exposure.
  • Applicant-facing waits need their own timers and nudges, since 'awaiting a document from the customer' is the most common stall and it has a churn cost.
  • Every wait needs a timeout with a defined next action. A case that can wait forever will, and it will be discovered by a customer complaint rather than by a dashboard.
  • Emit progress to the applicant, because opaque multi-day onboarding is the single biggest driver of abandonment in this flow.
Model answer: A durable workflow per application, where the workflow code is deterministic and every external call is a retryable activity, so the case survives restarts and a three-day wait costs a database row rather than a thread. I would model the case as an explicit state machine rather than a single pending flag, because awaiting-applicant and awaiting-third-party are operationally completely different and collapsing them hides which stalls you can actually do something about. Independent verifications fan out in parallel and join, since the registry lookup and the ID check have nothing to do with each other. The important modeling decision is that partial completion is normal: most applications clear most checks, so the state has to express verified-except-for-X, and that is what makes conditional approval possible — let them transact within a bounded limit while a slow check finishes. Every wait gets a timeout and a defined next action, because a case that can wait forever will, and you will hear about it from the customer rather than from a dashboard.

Q8 explain & apply crux

How do you evaluate this pipeline, and what does a good decision even mean here?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • Two distinct error types with completely different costs: approving a fraudulent business, and rejecting or stalling a legitimate one.
  • The first is a regulatory and financial loss; the second is churn and is far more common, which makes the honest tradeoff a business decision rather than a modeling one.
  • Ground truth on approvals is delayed and partial: you learn about fraud months later, if ever, and you almost never learn about the good businesses you wrongly rejected.
  • So instrument the rejection path deliberately — sample rejected applications for human re-review, because otherwise false rejections are invisible by construction.
  • Measure extraction and verification separately. Extraction accuracy is checkable against human transcription; verification correctness is not, and reporting one number for the pipeline hides which half is failing.
  • Track auto-clear rate, human-touch rate, and time-to-decision at p50 and p99, since p99 is where the customer experience actually lives.
  • Per-jurisdiction and per-document-type breakdowns, because an aggregate hides that one country's registry has been failing for two weeks.
  • The silent failure is a stale verification source: a registry starts returning empty rather than erroring, everything auto-clears, and the pipeline looks healthier than ever.
  • Guard that with canary lookups on known entities and an alert on any sudden shift in pass rate, because an improving pass rate with no deploy is a warning, not a win.
Model answer: There are two errors with very different costs: approving a fraudulent business, which is a regulatory and financial loss, and rejecting or stalling a legitimate one, which is churn and is far more common. The measurement problem is that ground truth is asymmetric — fraud surfaces months later if at all, and you essentially never learn about the good businesses you wrongly turned away. So I would deliberately sample rejections for human re-review, because false rejections are invisible by construction otherwise. Extraction and verification get measured separately, since extraction accuracy is checkable against human transcription and verification correctness is not, and one combined number hides which half is broken. Break everything out per jurisdiction and document type, because the aggregate hides one country's registry failing for a fortnight. And the silent failure I would guard hardest is a source that starts returning empty instead of erroring: everything auto-clears, pass rate improves, and the pipeline looks healthier than it has ever been. Canary lookups on known entities catch it; a rising pass rate with no deploy is a warning.

Q9 explain & apply crux

Staff-level wrap-up: what breaks, what does it cost, and how does this evolve?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • Vendor cost is the operating constraint. Cache verification results per entity with a declared freshness policy, and skip re-verification of a parent company already resolved for another application.
  • Order the checks by cost and by information gain: run the cheap disqualifying checks first so an application that will fail does not incur the expensive ownership walk.
  • That ordering is worth real money and is invisible in the architecture diagram, which is exactly why it is worth saying out loud.
  • Vendor concentration is the availability risk: a single registry aggregator going down stalls every application in that jurisdiction. Have a fallback source or an explicit degraded mode.
  • Retention and deletion are contractual, not optional. Identity documents attract deletion obligations that conflict with the retention obligation on the decision, and the resolution is usually to keep the decision record and the hash while purging the image.
  • That conflict is worth naming: 'keep the evidence forever' and 'delete their passport scan on request' are both real requirements and they need an explicit reconciliation.
  • Fraud adapts. A document template that passes today will be forged tomorrow, so extraction and validity checks need a refresh loop rather than a one-time build.
  • The evolution is toward reuse: a verified entity graph shared across applications, so the tenth business owned by the same parent verifies almost instantly and the cost curve bends.
  • Watch for the perverse incentive in auto-clear rate — it is trivially improved by lowering the bar, so it must always be reported next to the fraud and false-rejection numbers.
Model answer: Vendor spend is the operating constraint, so the highest-leverage work is caching verification results per entity with a declared freshness policy and ordering checks by cost and information gain — run the cheap disqualifying checks before the expensive ownership walk, which is worth real money and is completely invisible on the architecture diagram. The availability risk is vendor concentration: one registry aggregator going down stalls every application in a jurisdiction, so I would want a fallback or an explicit degraded mode rather than discovering it live. The tension I would name deliberately is retention versus deletion — the decision record must survive for years while the applicant can ask for their passport scan to be deleted, and the usual reconciliation is keeping the decision and the hash while purging the image. Longer term this evolves toward a shared verified entity graph, so the tenth business under the same parent verifies almost instantly. And I would be careful with auto-clear rate as a metric, because it improves beautifully by lowering the bar, so it only means anything reported beside fraud and false-rejection numbers.
