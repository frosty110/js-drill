Driving, Scoping & Deep-Dive Selection

A strong candidate steers the interview instead of waiting to be led — proposing the next step, narrating tradeoffs, and checking in. This section covers driving the conversation, scoping the problem out loud, stating assumptions, and choosing the one or two components worth a deep dive.

The System Design Interview 16 questions

Results carried by this link

Key ideas

  • You are expected to DRIVE: propose the next step and narrate your reasoning rather than going silent or asking the interviewer what to do next.
  • Scope explicitly and out loud — state what you're INCLUDING and EXCLUDING (e.g. 'core feed now, defer notifications and search') to buy time for depth.
  • When the interviewer won't give a number, state a reasonable assumption ('I'll assume ~100M DAU'), invite correction, and keep moving.
  • Pick a deep dive where the interesting scaling/consistency challenge lives (feed fan-out, ID generation, the matching engine) — not a trivial CRUD box.
  • Ask before you dive: confirm the interviewer wants that deep dive before spending ten minutes on it, and read their cues for where to steer.
  • Time-box every step; don't rat-hole on one detail or try to design everything at equal depth.
  • Interviewers are reading for structured thinking, prioritization, collaboration, and confidence with ambiguity — not for you needing hand-holding.

Q1 multiple choice

After you've sketched the high-level design, the interviewer goes quiet. What's the strongest move?

  1. A Wait silently until they tell you which part to work on next
  2. B Propose the next step yourself and check in — 'I'd like to deep-dive the feed generation next; does that work?' correct
  3. C Ask the interviewer to pick the component they want you to design
  4. D Start listing every possible feature you could add to look thorough

Why: Driving means proposing the next step and confirming, not waiting to be led or offloading the decision back to the interviewer. Silence and 'what should I do next?' both signal you need hand-holding, which is exactly what the interviewer is checking for.

Q2 multiple choice

What does 'explicit scoping' mean in a system design interview?

  1. A Estimating the exact QPS and storage before designing anything
  2. B Designing every feature the real product has so nothing is missed
  3. C Stating out loud what you will include and what you'll defer, and getting agreement correct
  4. D Picking the database technology before drawing the architecture

Why: Scoping is naming what's in and what's out ('core feed now, defer notifications and search') and confirming it, which frees you to go deep on what matters. It's a prioritization act, distinct from capacity estimation or technology selection.

Q3 multiple choice

Why do strong candidates narrow the scope early instead of covering the full product?

  1. A Because a narrower scope lets you demonstrate real depth in the limited time correct
  2. B Because interviewers deduct points for mentioning extra features
  3. C Because the interviewer always wants the smallest possible system
  4. D Because scope has no effect on how the design is evaluated

Why: Deliberately deferring peripheral features buys you the time to go deep on the core, which is where the signal is. Trying to design everything at equal depth in ~45 minutes leaves nothing finished — over-scoping is a common failure, not thoroughness.

Q4 multiple choice

The interviewer won't commit to a user count when you ask. What should you do?

  1. A Refuse to proceed until they give you an exact number
  2. B Skip estimation entirely since you don't have a number
  3. C Design for the absolute maximum scale imaginable to be safe
  4. D State a reasonable assumption like '~100M DAU', note it's an assumption, and move on correct

Why: When a number isn't handed to you, stating a defensible assumption and inviting correction keeps the interview moving and shows you can operate under ambiguity. Stalling, over-engineering for extreme scale, or skipping sizing altogether all read worse than a clearly-labeled assumption.

Q5 multiple choice

You're designing a URL shortener. Which component is the best deep-dive choice?

  1. A The user login form and its validation logic
  2. B The unique short-ID generation and its collision/scaling strategy correct
  3. C The HTTP status codes returned on redirect
  4. D The CSS layout of the landing page

Why: The deep dive should target where the interesting engineering challenge lives — for a URL shortener that's generating unique, short, non-colliding IDs at scale. Auth forms, status codes, and page layout are trivial CRUD/presentation concerns that don't demonstrate systems depth.

Q6 multiple choice

For 'Design Twitter', which area typically makes the richest deep dive?

  1. A The password reset email flow
  2. B The account settings page
  3. C The feed fan-out — push vs pull vs hybrid on write and read correct
  4. D The choice of web framework for the API layer

Why: Feed fan-out is Twitter's defining scaling and consistency challenge, so it best showcases depth and tradeoff reasoning. Settings pages, email flows, and framework choices are peripheral and carry little design signal.

Q7 multiple choice

Before spending ten minutes drilling into the matching engine of a ride-share design, what should you do?

  1. A Confirm the interviewer wants that deep dive, or ask if there's an area they'd rather you focus on correct
  2. B Just start — asking wastes valuable time
  3. C Deep-dive every component briefly instead of choosing one
  4. D Ask the interviewer to grade your high-level design first

Why: Checking 'would it be useful to go deep on the matching engine, or is there an area you'd prefer?' keeps you collaborative and avoids sinking ten minutes into something the interviewer didn't want. It also surfaces their cues about where the signal they care about lives.

Q8 multiple choice

The interviewer interrupts your feed deep-dive with 'How would you handle the data storage here?' What does that signal?

  1. A You're failing and they're trying to rescue you
  2. B The interview is over and they're wrapping up early
  3. C They're steering you toward an area they want to see — follow the hint and go there correct
  4. D They want you to ignore it and finish your current point first

Why: A pointed question is a steer toward what the interviewer wants to evaluate, and following it shows responsiveness and collaboration. Ignoring or deflecting an explicit hint is one of the clearer negative signals in the loop.

Q9 multiple choice

You've spent eight minutes perfecting one caching detail and haven't covered the rest of the design. What's the fix?

  1. A Keep going until the caching layer is completely airtight
  2. B Restart the whole design to rebalance your time
  3. C Drop the deep dive entirely to make up the lost minutes
  4. D Recognize the rat-hole, time-box it, and move the overall design forward correct

Why: Rat-holing on a single detail burns the clock and starves the rest of the arc, so the fix is to consciously time-box and move on. Restarting or abandoning the deep dive over-corrects; the skill is proportional attention across the whole design.

Q10 multiple choice

Which set of signals are interviewers primarily reading for during scoping and driving?

  1. A Memorization of specific database vendors and version numbers
  2. B Structured thinking, prioritization, collaboration, and confidence with ambiguity correct
  3. C Typing speed and the neatness of the diagram
  4. D Knowledge of the interviewer's own company's internal architecture

Why: The scoping/driving phase is where you show you can structure a problem, prioritize, collaborate, and handle ambiguity without hand-holding. Vendor trivia, diagram aesthetics, and insider knowledge are not what's being measured.

Q11 multiple choice

What's the best way to choose which one or two components to deep-dive?

  1. A Pick the component that's central to the problem AND has the most interesting scaling or consistency challenge correct
  2. B Pick whichever component you personally find easiest to explain
  3. C Pick the component the interviewer hasn't mentioned yet to surprise them
  4. D Deep-dive all components equally so nothing is neglected

Why: The ideal deep dive is both central to the problem and rich in tradeoffs, so it maximizes demonstrated depth on something that matters. Choosing the easy box, an off-topic one, or spreading attention evenly all lower the signal you generate.

Q12 multiple choice

Which behavior is an anti-pattern in the driving/scoping phase?

  1. A Narrating your reasoning as you propose the next step
  2. B Stating an assumption when the interviewer withholds a number
  3. C Trying to design every component at equal depth so the arc never finishes correct
  4. D Confirming scope before starting the high-level design

Why: Designing everything at equal depth is over-scoping — it guarantees you run out of time without demonstrating depth anywhere. Narrating, stating assumptions, and confirming scope are all the correct driving behaviors.

Q13 multiple choice

How should you narrate the transition from high-level design into a deep dive?

  1. A Silently erase the diagram and start drawing the new component
  2. B Say what you're locking in and what you'll dive into next, then confirm it works for the interviewer correct
  3. C Ask the interviewer to summarize the design so far for you
  4. D Announce you're done and wait for the next question

Why: Voicing 'I'll lock in this high-level design and go deep on X — does that work?' keeps the interviewer synchronized and shows you're driving with intent. Going silent or handing the summary back to them both drop the thread of a narrated, collaborative process.

Q14 explain & apply

You hear 'Design Instagram.' Walk me through how you'd scope the problem out loud in the first few minutes.

Points a strong answer hits:

  • Clarifies the feature surface — e.g. photo upload + home feed vs. also stories, DMs, search, explore.
  • States an explicit scope, naming what's INCLUDED and what's DEFERRED (e.g. 'core photo post + home feed; defer stories and DMs').
  • Confirms the scope with the interviewer rather than assuming unilaterally.
  • Ties scope to the non-functional forces — read-heavy, scale, latency — that will shape the design.
  • Frames the narrowing as buying time to go deep, not as cutting corners.
Model answer: I'd first clarify the feature surface — is this photo upload and the home feed, or do we also want stories, DMs, search, and explore? Then I'd propose an explicit scope out loud: 'I'll focus on core photo posting and the home feed, and defer stories, DMs, and search unless you'd rather I cover those' — and confirm that works. I'd tie it to the non-functional profile, noting Instagram is heavily read-skewed at large scale with tight feed-latency expectations, since that's what drives the architecture. Narrowing early isn't cutting corners; it's what buys me the time to go deep on the part that carries the real signal.

Q15 explain & apply

How do you decide which component to deep-dive, and how do you make sure it's what the interviewer wants?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • Picks a component that is central to the problem, not a peripheral CRUD box.
  • Chooses where the interesting scaling or consistency challenge lives (e.g. feed fan-out, ID generation, matching engine).
  • Explicitly asks/confirms before spending ten minutes on it.
  • Reads the interviewer's cues and is willing to redirect to the area they steer toward.
  • Time-boxes the deep dive so wrap-up still happens.
  • Frames it collaboratively and narrates the tradeoffs while diving.
Model answer: I look for the component that's both central to the problem and rich in tradeoffs — the feed fan-out for Twitter, ID generation for a URL shortener, the matching engine for ride-share — because that's where I can show the most depth. Before I sink ten minutes in, I confirm: 'Would it be useful to go deep on the feed generation, or is there an area you'd rather I focus on?' and I watch for cues — if they nudge me toward storage or consistency, I follow that steer. Then I time-box the dive and narrate the tradeoffs as I go, so I still leave room to wrap up with bottlenecks and next steps.

Q16 explain & apply

What does it mean to 'drive' a system design interview, and why does it matter to the interviewer?

Points a strong answer hits:

  • Driving means proposing the next step yourself rather than waiting to be told.
  • Involves narrating your reasoning and checking in, keeping the interviewer synchronized.
  • Includes stating assumptions when numbers are withheld and moving forward under ambiguity.
  • Requires time-boxing and keeping the whole arc moving rather than rat-holing.
  • Signals seniority — the interviewer is checking you don't need hand-holding, especially at staff/senior-staff level.
  • Stays collaborative — driving is not steamrolling; you read and respond to interviewer cues.
Model answer: Driving means I own the flow — I propose the next step, narrate why, and check in, rather than going silent and waiting to be led. When the interviewer withholds a number I state a reasonable assumption and keep moving, and I time-box each step so I'm not rat-holing while the clock runs. It matters because at a staff or senior-staff bar they're specifically checking that I can run a hard, ambiguous problem without hand-holding. But driving isn't steamrolling — I stay collaborative, reading their cues and redirecting when they steer me toward an area they care about.
